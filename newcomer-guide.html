1. Welcome and Group Overview

1.1 Welcome to LOMS

Welcome to the Laboratory of Optoelectronic Materials Simulation (LOMS)! This guide is for new graduate students, undergraduates and research assistants. It explains what the group works on and why, what you need to learn, which tools you will use, and what a typical first year looks like. All research content here comes from the group website and published papers. For specific group rules, follow what the PI and senior students tell you.

1.2 Who We Are

According to the group website, LOMS is a computational/theoretical laboratory at the intersection of materials science, quantum chemistry and artificial intelligence. It focuses on mechanistic studies of electronic structure and excited-state properties of optoelectronic materials, and uses machine learning models and AI methods to develop high-performance organic optoelectronic materials for intelligent displays. Most recently, the group has been working on discovering potential new molecules and materials through quantum chemistry computation and database technologies such as the OLEDLibrary platform (https://www.oledlibrary.com ).

The Research page adds that the group's work covers the development of optoelectronic materials and the optimization of device performance. This includes the structural design of organic/inorganic electroluminescent materials, organic electrochromic materials, and the simulation and optimization of optoelectronic devices. Recent work has concentrated on high-throughput computation and screening of OLED materials and on AI-assisted development of high-performance blue OLED materials, in collaboration with experimental groups working on organic optoelectronic synthesis and perovskite light-emitting devices.

2. Research Directions

Based on the website's research description and the titles and journals of the 74 papers (2016–2026), the group's work falls into five directions. Below, "*" marks a corresponding author as shown on the website. Numbers in brackets are the paper numbers on the website's Publications page.

2.1 Direction 1: Excited-State Mechanisms and Molecular Design of Organic Emitters (MR-TADF / TADF / Hot Exciton)

Scientific questions: Starting from electronic structure and excited-state properties, how can we understand and control the emission color, color purity (full width at half maximum, FWHM) and exciton utilization of organic emitters? Typical questions are: where the narrowband emission of nitrogen/carbonyl (N/C=O) multi-resonance thermally activated delayed fluorescence (MR-TADF) emitters comes from; how steric effects and vibrational coupling broaden spectra; how donor/acceptor structure tunes the S0/S1 transition; how high-lying reverse intersystem crossing (RISC) works in "hot exciton" channels; and how vibrational coupling affects reorganization energy in π-isoelectronic frameworks.

Representative work:

  • [50] Origins of Narrowband Emission in Nitrogen/Carbonyl Multiresonance Thermally Activated Delayed Fluorescence Emitters: Steric Locks and Vibrational Coupling Effects. Journal of Physical Chemistry Letters, 2024. (Yanan Zhu*; according to the website news, the related cover design was selected as a JPCL Front Cover)
  • [62] Chemical Strategies for Modifying Carbonyl/Nitrogen-Based MR-TADF Materials toward Narrowband Emission. Advanced Optical Materials, 2025. (Yanan Zhu*; according to the website news, the related cover design was selected as an Advanced Optical Materials Front Cover)
  • [34] Donor–Acceptor–Donor "Hot Exciton" Triads for High Reverse Intersystem Crossing in OLEDs. Advanced Optical Materials, 2022. (with the Corminboeuf group, EPFL)
  • [49] Molecules Screening and Electronic Effects of N/C==O-based MR-TADF Materials with Narrow Blue Emission. Chinese Journal of Luminesence, 2024.
  • [61] Steric effects and electronic manipulation of multiple donors on S0/S1 transition of Dn-A emitters. Chinese Chemical Letters, 2025.

  • [32] Narrowband Deep-Blue Multi-Resonance Induced Thermally Activated Delayed Fluorescence: Insights from the Theoretical Molecular Design. Molecules, 2022.

2.2 Direction 2: AI-Driven Materials Design, Databases and Intelligent Computing Platforms

Scientific questions: How can databases, machine learning, and generative or language models speed up property prediction, high-throughput screening and inverse design of OLED and other optoelectronic materials? Topics include interpretable fragment-based machine learning, gradient-boosting models for property prediction, a unified language model for OLED molecular design, generating 3D molecular geometries from chemical syntax (e.g., SMILES-like representations), and building the OLEDLibrary database of OLED materials.

Representative work:

  • [74] OLEDLM: A Unified Language Model for OLED Molecular Design. arXiv preprint, 2026.
  • [67] MolSculpt: Sculpting 3D Molecular Geometries from Chemical Syntax. arXiv preprint, 2025.
  • [73] Decoding donor/acceptor hierarchy in DAD triads via fragment-centric machine learning. Journal of Materials Informatics, 2026. (Yanan Zhu*)
  • [65] AI-Driven Accelerated Discovery of High-Performance Perovskite Quantum Dots Via Predictive LightGBM Modeling. The Journal of Physical Chemistry Letters, 2025. (Yanan Zhu* et al.)
  • [68] Virtual screening of CBP derivatives for OLED applications towards evaluation of substitution effects on electronic and optical properties. Optical Materials: X, 2026.
  • [15] Computational screening and molecular design of anthracene-based semiconductors. Organic Electronics, 2018.
  • Platform: the OLEDLibrary database (https://www.oledlibrary.com ; launched November 2024, still being updated).

2.3 Direction 3: Multiscale Simulation of Perovskite/Organic Interfaces and Optoelectronic Devices

Scientific questions: How do the orientation and morphology of organic molecules on perovskite and other inorganic surfaces affect interfacial charge transfer and stability? How can theory guide the choice of interface layers and donor/acceptor materials?

Representative work:

  • [38] Manipulating Interfacial Performance of TPBi-FAPbI3 Perovskite Interfaces Based on TPBi Orientations: A Theoretical Perspective. Journal of Physical Chemistry C, 2023. (Yanan Zhu*)

  • [45] Theoretical investigation of benzodithiophene-based donor molecules in organic solar cells: from structural optimization to performance metrics. Royal Society of Chemistry Advances, 2024.

2.4 Direction 4: Lanthanide/Metal Complex Luminescence, X-ray Scintillators, and Combined Theory–Experiment Studies

Scientific questions: What controls the excited-state lifetimes, energy transfer, and luminescence/scintillation mechanisms of lanthanide (Eu, Tb, Gd, Yb, etc.) complexes, Cu(I)/Ag(I) complexes and MOFs? How can ligand engineering improve OLED and X-ray imaging performance? Much of this work is done with experimental groups (such as the Utochnikova group at MSU), so calculations and experiments check each other.

Representative work:

  • [66] Enhanced efficiency of solution-processed lanthanide-based OLEDs via ligand engineering: Tuning excited-state lifetime and charge transport with Se-modified ligand. Chinese Chemical Letters, 2025. (Yanan Zhu* et al.)
  • [53] Ultrabright and Water-Stable Eu(III)-Based MOF Scintillators Sensitized by Dual-Antenna Ligands for Real-Time and Underwater X-Ray Imaging. Angewandte Chemie International Edition, 2025. (Yanan Zhu* et al.)
  • [43] Thermally Activated Delayed Fluorescent Ag(I) Complexes for Highly Efficient Scintillation and High-Resolution X-Ray Imaging. Advanced Functional Materials, 2024.
  • [63] Cathodoluminescence degradation mechanism for europium coordination compound. Journal of Luminescence, 2025.
  • [72] Cathodo- and X-ray excited luminescence of europium tri- and tetracarboxybenzoates and phenanthroline. Physical Chemistry Chemical Physics, 2026.

2.5 Direction 5: Collaborative Studies on Organic Electronic Materials and Devices (OLED Hosts/Transport Materials, Electrochromics, Charge Transport, Energy Storage)

Scientific questions: Working with synthesis and device groups, the group uses calculations to explain structure–property relationships: dipole orientation of OLED host materials; hole-transport materials and device lifetime; the optical response of electrochromic and electrofluorochromic molecules and polymers; charge-transport mechanisms and mobility in organic semiconductors; and the structure–capacity relationship of organic electrode materials for energy storage.

  • [20] Revealing the mechanism of contrasting charge transport properties for phenyl and thienyl substituent organic semiconductors. Physical Chemistry Chemical Physics, 2019.
  • [8] Understanding the mechanism of improvement in practical specific capacity using halogen substituted anthraquinones as cathode materials in lithium batteries. Electrochimica Acta, 2017.

2.6 Choosing a Project

These directions are closely linked. Direction 1 supplies physical mechanisms and descriptors, Direction 2 scales them up with data and AI, and Directions 3–5 apply the computational methods to a wider range of materials and devices. A newcomer's project is usually chosen based on the PI's current grants and the student's background.

3. Background Knowledge

The topics below are split into "core for everyone" and "by direction". You do not need to learn everything at once; Section 5 gives a step-by-step roadmap.

3.1 Quantum Chemistry and Electronic Structure (core for everyone)

  • Basic quantum mechanics: Schrödinger equation, operators and eigenvalues, harmonic oscillator, angular momentum and spin, perturbation theory, variational principle.
  • Molecular orbital theory and Hartree–Fock (HF): Born–Oppenheimer approximation, Slater determinants, self-consistent field (SCF), basis sets (Pople, Dunning, def2) and basis-set convergence.
  • Density functional theory (DFT): Hohenberg–Kohn theorems, Kohn–Sham equations, "Jacob's ladder" of functionals (LDA/GGA/hybrid/range-separated hybrid/double hybrid), dispersion corrections (e.g., D3/D4), solvent models (PCM/SMD).
  • Excited-state methods: TDDFT and the Tamm–Dancoff approximation (TDA), range-separated functionals and charge-transfer states, natural transition orbitals (NTOs) and hole–electron analysis; when MR-TADF systems need higher-level methods (e.g., ADC(2), CC2, SCS-CC2, STEOM-DLPNO-CCSD).
  • Spin multiplicity and excitons: singlet/triplet states, S1–T1 gap (ΔEST), locally excited (LE), charge-transfer (CT) and hybridized local and charge-transfer (HLCT) states.

3.2 Photophysics and Luminescence Mechanisms (key for Directions 1 and 4)

  • Jablonski diagram and basic processes: absorption, fluorescence, phosphorescence, internal conversion (IC), intersystem crossing (ISC), reverse intersystem crossing (RISC).
  • Spin–orbit coupling (SOC): El-Sayed rules, heavy-atom effect, computing SOC matrix elements.
  • TADF / MR-TADF / hot-exciton mechanisms: design principles for small ΔEST, the multi-resonance effect, RISC channels through high-lying triplets (Tn).
  • Rate theory: Fermi's golden rule, Marcus theory, Strickler–Berg relation (radiative rates); basic idea of rate calculations from vibrational correlation functions.
  • Vibronic coupling and spectral line shapes: Franck–Condon principle, Huang–Rhys factors, reorganization-energy decomposition, Duschinsky rotation, vibrationally resolved spectra and FWHM. These are central to the narrowband emission papers ([50][52][62]).
  • Lanthanide and metal complex luminescence (Direction 4): antenna effect, ligand-to-metal energy transfer, f–f transitions, basics of scintillation.

3.3 Organic Optoelectronic Materials and Device Physics (everyone should know the basics; key for Directions 1 and 5)

  • OLED device structure (anode / hole injection and transport layers / emitting layer / electron transport layer / cathode), host–guest doping.
  • Breakdown of external quantum efficiency (EQE): charge balance, exciton utilization, photoluminescence quantum yield (PLQY), outcoupling efficiency (linked to horizontal emitting-dipole orientation).
  • Color coordinates (CIE), FWHM, efficiency roll-off, device lifetime (LT95/LT50, etc.).
  • Charge transport in organic semiconductors: hopping model, Marcus charge-transfer rate, transfer integrals and reorganization energy, mobility.
  • Related devices: organic solar cells (OSCs), organic field-effect transistors (OFETs), organic photodetectors, electrochromic devices, perovskite light-emitting and photovoltaic devices.

3.4 Periodic Systems and Multiscale Simulation (Direction 3, as needed)

  • Plane-wave/pseudopotential DFT, band structure and density of states, surface and interface models (slab models), work function and energy-level alignment.
  • Molecular dynamics (MD): force fields, ensembles, integrators, simulation of film and interface morphology; basic ideas of QM/MM and multiscale modeling.

3.5 Machine Learning and Artificial Intelligence (key for Direction 2; everyone should know the basics)

  • Basics: supervised learning (regression/classification), cross-validation, overfitting and regularization, feature engineering and model interpretability (e.g., SHAP).
  • Classical models: linear models, random forests, gradient-boosted trees (e.g., LightGBM, see [65]).
  • Molecular representations: SMILES/SELFIES, molecular fingerprints, molecular descriptors, fragment-based representations (see [73]), 3D geometry representations.
  • Deep learning: neural networks and backpropagation, graph neural networks (GNNs), Transformers.
  • Generative models and large language models: molecular generation, conditional generation and inverse design, chemical language models ([67] and [74] cover generating 3D molecules from chemical syntax and a unified language model for OLED molecular design).
  • Data engineering: collecting and cleaning data from literature and databases, building and versioning datasets (relevant to the OLEDLibrary database work).

7. Research Conduct and Daily Life in the Group

This section gives general advice. Specific group rules (times, places, platforms, server names, etc.) are set by the PI. Please check them with the PI or senior students after you join.

7.1 Data Management

  • Folder structure: Use a separate folder for each project, organized by system/method/date. Keep inputs, outputs, analysis scripts and figures apart, and add a README describing each folder and its calculation settings.
  • Reproducibility: Record software versions, methods (functional/basis set/solvent model/convergence criteria) and key keywords. Put analysis scripts in a Git repository.
  • Backups: Keep important data in at least two places (server plus personal or group storage). (Check with the PI for the group data storage and backup locations. Data handover when leaving the group: hand over raw data and figures to the group storage node.)
  • Electronic lab notebook: Record progress and problems daily or weekly (Markdown, OneNote, Notion, etc.), prepare weekly report slides using the group template, and send them to the PI before Sunday each week.
  • Database data: For data from OLEDLibrary or other databases, follow the source's copyright and citation rules.

7.2 Using Computing Resources

  • Do not run large calculations on login nodes. Request only the cores, memory and wall time you need, and do not hold resources for long periods.
  • Test large campaigns (e.g., high-throughput screening) on a small sample first. Clean up temporary files (e.g., .chk, .gbw, scratch files) and watch your disk quota.
  • Never share accounts or passwords, and never put server credentials in public code repositories.
  • Use commercial software only within its license. Do not copy or distribute installers or license files.

7.3 Group Meetings and Presentations

  • Group meetings usually include progress reports and literature presentations. In your talk, cover question – method – results – next steps, and label methods and units on figures.
  • Update the PI or your mentor on progress every week. If you have been stuck for more than 2–3 days, ask for help.
  • Please contact the PI for the time, venue and format of group meetings. The presentation rotation schedule is in the internal documents.

7.4 Paper Writing and Authorship

  • Before writing, agree with the PI on the main storyline and key figures. The main contributor writes the first draft and revises it based on the PI's feedback.
  • Authorship follows actual contributions and common community standards (e.g., ICMJE criteria, the CRediT taxonomy). For joint work with partner institutions, agree on author order, corresponding authors and affiliations in advance.
  • Get the PI's approval before submitting a paper, posting a preprint (e.g., on arXiv), giving a conference talk or releasing data publicly.

7.5 Academic Integrity

  • Never fabricate, falsify or plagiarize. Report calculation settings and failed results honestly, and do not present data selectively.
  • Always cite other people's work. If you use AI tools for writing or coding, follow journal and university policies, and you remain responsible for the correctness of the content.
  • Keep unpublished data and results from collaborative projects confidential; do not share them without permission.
  • Follow the academic regulations for students at Shenzhen MSU-BIT University and at your degree-granting institution.

7.6 Daily Matters

  • For leave and for conference and travel reimbursement procedures, please contact the PI.

8. FAQ

8.1 I have a chemistry/materials background but no programming experience. Can I do well?

Yes. Spend the first month on Linux and Python basics, starting with small tasks such as parsing output files and making plots. The group's research is itself a mix of materials science, quantum chemistry and AI, so a variety of backgrounds is normal.

8.2 I come from computer science or mathematics and have no chemistry background. What should I do?

First learn the basic ideas of organic and structural chemistry (conjugation, donors/acceptors, molecular orbitals), then read Lewars's introductory textbook and the photophysics chapters of Turro. You can also start with Direction 2 (AI and databases) and use data and models to build an intuitive feel for molecular properties.

8.3 My calculation won't converge or keeps crashing. What should I do?

Read the error message at the end of the output file. Check that the starting structure is reasonable, the charge and spin multiplicity are correct, and the method and basis-set keywords are spelled correctly. Then look at the software manual and resources such as Sobereva's blog. If you still cannot solve it, ask a senior student or the PI, and bring your input file, the error message and what you have already tried.

8.4 My TDDFT excitation energies do not match experiment. Is that normal?

To some extent, yes. TDDFT results depend strongly on the functional, especially for charge-transfer states and MR-TADF systems (which often need higher-level methods). Benchmark functionals, focus on trends rather than absolute values, and state the method's limitations in your paper.

8.5 Which quantum chemistry program should I use?

It depends on your project and the licenses the group has. In general, Gaussian or ORCA work for routine DFT/TDDFT; ORCA or Q-Chem for SOC and high-level excited states; and fast methods such as xtb for high-throughput pre-screening.

8.6 How much data does a machine learning model need?

It depends on the task and the model's complexity. For small datasets (a few hundred samples), use descriptors with classical models such as gradient-boosted trees and strict cross-validation. Deep learning and generative models usually need larger datasets or pretraining. Data quality and how you split the data (to avoid leakage) often matter more than the choice of model.

8.7 How long until my first paper?

It depends on the project and your effort; there is no fixed timeline. Focus on mastering the methods and producing reliable data, and the paper will follow. Publication requirements for degrees follow university rules.

8.8 Who should I ask when I have a problem?

Ask your mentor or senior students about technical problems first, and talk to the PI directly about project direction and progress. For personal or mental-health concerns, you can also go to the PI or the relevant university services. PI email: zhuyn@smbu.edu.cn.
